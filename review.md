# Peer Review: Multi-Objective RL Alignment for Mental Health LLMs: Dynamic Reward Weighting and Free Safety Constraints

**Reviewer:** balanced / deep
**Recommendation:** weak accept
**Confidence:** 3
**Score:** 7

## Summary of contributions
The paper trains an instruction-tuned 1.5B language model with GRPO under a constrained multi-objective formulation that decomposes counselling response quality into Guidance, Informativeness, and Empathy, with a Lagrangian safety penalty and a Dynamic Reward Weighting (DRW) module. The authors evaluate eight conditions on CounselBench-Eval and replicate on a disjoint CounselChat slice. Their two most useful empirical observations are that the safety Lagrangian costs essentially nothing (0.25% composite reward) and that the apparent "ceiling" on empathy is a budget allocation artefact, not a reward-model limit (M8 raises empathy from 4.213 to 4.918 with full budget).

## Strengths
1. **Clean experimental design.** The eight-condition matrix is well thought through: M2 vs. M3 isolates fixed-weight strategy, M5 vs. M7 isolates the safety constraint, M7-d005/d020 sweeps the threshold, and M8 vs. M9 isolates the safety effect on empathy. Reading the conditions table, I could predict what each ablation would tell me, which is what good design looks like.
2. **Honest negative result.** The authors explicitly report that DRW collapses to equal weights and does not significantly outperform M3 (p=0.442). They neither bury this nor over-spin it; instead they reframe it as "the adaptive method discovers that equal weighting is near-optimal here". This kind of intellectual honesty is rare and welcome.
3. **Pre-registration and statistics.** Hypotheses H1–H4 are pre-registered, paired Wilcoxon with Holm-Bonferroni correction is the right test for the multi-comparison setting, and Cliff's delta + Wilson CIs are appropriate effect-size and proportion summaries. The 5% non-inferiority margin is declared up front rather than discovered after the fact.
4. **Appropriate scope.** The 1.5B parameter budget is justified by privacy / on-device constraints rather than a brute "small is good" claim; this matches a real deployment concern in the mental health setting.
5. **Cross-benchmark replication.** The CounselChat replication (276 questions, 29 topics, disjoint from CounselBench-Eval) materially strengthens the headline numbers.

## Weaknesses
1. **Reward-model evaluation is the only quality signal** (severity: high). Every quality claim in the paper is mediated by four DistilRoBERTa scorers that the authors themselves trained. The cross-system comparison in Table 4 is particularly affected: humans scoring lower than M7 across every dimension is implausible at face value and the authors correctly caveat it as an instrument bias, but the caveat does not address H1–H3, which are also reward-model claims. H4 is declared deferred, but this is the hypothesis that would have validated H1–H3 by anchoring to humans, and its absence is treated more lightly than it deserves.
2. **Model-size comparison is misleading without scaling controls** (severity: medium). The 1.5B vs. 70B vs. 1.8T narrative is rhetorically forceful but the comparison conflates parameter count with training data, instruction-tuning recipe, and access to the reward signal we trained against. A within-family scaling sweep (Qwen2.5-0.5B, 1.5B, 7B trained with the same recipe) would either cement or kill the "specialized small model wins" claim; without it, this is a single-data-point story.
3. **Method section is light on the DRW collapse mechanism** (severity: medium). The paper reports that adaptive weights converge to ~(0.33, 0.33, 0.33) but does not characterise the convergence dynamics. Is this a property of the per-axis ceiling formulation, the EMA rate, or the simplex projection? A 5-line analysis or a learning-curve plot of w_G(t), w_I(t), w_E(t) would be more informative than the prose claim.
4. **Empathy-budget ablation does not address the obvious next question** (severity: medium). Showing that M8 reaches empathy 4.918 with the full budget is informative, but the open question is the Pareto front: at intermediate empathy weights (say 0.5/0.25/0.25 or 0.7/0.15/0.15), what happens to guidance and informativeness? The current data lets the reader infer the endpoints but not the curvature, which is the operationally interesting object for practitioners.
5. **No human-rated qualitative analysis** (severity: medium). For a mental-health paper, I expected a small set of side-by-side response examples (e.g., 3 prompts × 3 conditions) so the reviewer can sanity-check whether the reward-score gaps actually correspond to subjectively better counselling. None are provided.
6. **Method-section pseudocode is missing** (severity: low). For a final manuscript, a 12-line algorithm block specifying GRPO step + Lagrangian update + DRW renormalization would substantially aid reproducibility.

## Specific comments
- Page 1, abstract: "1.5B-parameter M7 outperforms GPT-4, LLaMA3-70B, Gemini, and the human-counsellor responder set on every quality dimension" reads strongly; the "Under the same scoring instrument" qualifier helps but I'd recommend explicitly inserting the LLM-tilted-scorer caveat into the abstract itself.
- Section 3.2 (DRW): the per-axis headroom formula uses $\mu_k^{\max}=5.0$ as the upper bound, which makes the "moving toward whichever axis has more room" claim conceptually clean. Worth stating that this is the obvious choice but not necessarily the one that maximises hypervolume; a brief discussion would help.
- Section 3.3 (GRPO with safety Lagrangian): the dual gradient on $\lambda$ is the right tool here but the paper says nothing about how often the multiplier was actually active during training. Did $\lambda$ stay near zero, or did it spike when violations crossed $d$?
- Table 1: the highest composite (13.623) is achieved by M5 (no safety), not the proposed M7 (13.588). The narrative correctly handles this (safety is essentially free), but a reader skimming the table without reading the caption may briefly conclude M5 is the best system. A bolded "best safe condition" annotation would help.
- Table 2: the M5 row has a comma instead of an em-dash for "Threshold d" (intentional given the project's em-dash ban, but the result is a bare comma cell that looks like a typo; consider "—" replaced by "n/a" or the cell left blank).
- Figures 3–5 (rewards / effect sizes / empathy): the legend conventions are clean and the bars are accurately positioned, but Figures 4 and 5 would benefit from explicit Cohen's d or paired-test annotations on the bars where claims are made (e.g., "+0.705, p<0.0001" already on Fig 5; replicate on Fig 4 for the d=0.66 informativeness bar).
- Figure 7 (baselines): The four-system comparison is the most consequential plot in the paper; please consider showing 95% CIs on each bar so readers can read the gap relative to noise.
- Section 5 (results), H4 paragraph: appreciated that this is now explicitly deferred. Would strengthen by stating the cost in dollars or volunteer hours required to collect the missing ratings, so the next-iteration plan is concrete.

## Recommendation justification
The work makes two genuinely useful contributions to the small but growing literature on safety-constrained alignment for sensitive domains: a clean, repeatable demonstration that the Lagrangian safety constraint is (cost-wise) free, and a careful budget ablation that disentangles "empathy is hard" from "empathy gets less compute". The negative result on dynamic weighting is reported with rare honesty and the cross-benchmark replication is exactly the right move. The single biggest weakness is that every quality claim still rides on a reward-model instrument the authors trained, with no human-validation fold available; for a workshop submission this is acceptable so long as the limitation is loud, but I would not advance the paper to a top-tier main track without the H4 results that the authors themselves planned. Recommend acceptance to a workshop/short-track venue, conditional on tightening the abstract caveat and adding either a within-family scaling sweep or a small human-rated case study before camera-ready.

## Minor issues
- Title wraps with "Constraints" alone on the second line in some readers; consider rephrasing or letting LaTeX line-break naturally.
- Section 3 (Method) cites Shao et al. for GRPO once and then defines it; the GRPO advantage formula is restated in close to the original notation, which is fine, but a one-liner about why the per-prompt group baseline is the right baseline here (vs. PPO's value head) would help non-RL readers.
- Page 4 has two figures rendered top-of-page with no body text between them; consider reflowing so Figure 2 lands on a later page, or shrink one of the figures so prose can sit between them.
- Reference [11] (wu2024morlhf) is cited in the related-work themed section as multi-objective alignment; the actual paper title is "Fine-Grained Human Feedback Gives Better Rewards…", which is a related-but-distinct contribution. Consider one extra sentence to disambiguate.
- The bibliography is numbered in citation order (correct per `unsrtnat`), but [30] (Wilson 1927) appears before [31] (Gemini 2023) and [32] (GPT-4 2023) on the last page, which surprised me until I noticed the cite order. No action required.
